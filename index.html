Reading high-school grades · California, classes 1994–2025

Did high-school grades inflate, and does anyone still learn anything from them?

Nobody disputes that the grade point averages arriving at the University of California have risen. The open question is what the scale still communicates, and it splits into five questions that reasonable people weigh differently: whether grades have crowded against the top of the scale until one grade point distinguishes very little (compression); whether grades moved away from independent measures of the same students (criterion divergence); whether admission decisions now express distinctions the scale no longer states (selection divergence); whether a grade means the same thing at different schools and in different years (calibration); and whether the grade a student enters with still predicts what happens afterward (prediction). Each tab takes up one of these as a question, not a verdict, and hands the judgments that drive the answer — which years to compare, which students to count, what to accept as independent evidence of achievement — to the reader as controls.

Everything is built from the University’s published grade point averages for every California high school, graduating classes 1994 to 2025, set beside every school-level testing program the state has run since 1999, and from the University’s own record of who graduates. The rise these figures examine runs back decades — it neither starts nor breaks at any single policy year — and the long axes are drawn in full so that can be seen.

Compression · how much does a grade point still distinguish near the top of the scale?

Same school, different doors

Every California high school sends applicants to all nine undergraduate campuses. The students who apply to Merced are, on the whole, further down their own class than the students who apply to Berkeley. Comparing those slices inside one school in one year holds the school constant: whatever its teaching, its neighborhood and its actual achievement, they are the same for both slices. Whatever separates the slices is the reach of the pool, not the school.

The slices also start at different distances from the top of the scale, and the scale has a hard cap: the closer a pool already sits to it, the less room any rise has, and the less one grade point can still distinguish. This figure measures how far each slice moved against the room it had left. Compression of that kind is a loss of resolution whatever produced it — easier grading, heavier course weighting, or real improvement running out of headroom.

Selection divergence · does the scale still express the distinctions the admission process makes?

The crowded top

A grading scale is useful to the extent that it separates students. One way to measure how much separating power the high-school grade point average still carries, without first settling whether grades have risen, is to ask a narrower question: inside a single high school, how far above the students it turned away does the University’s admitted group sit?

That distance — admitted minus denied — is the separation the record actually contains, derived school by school from the published applicant and admitted averages and their counts. The older figure, admitted minus applicants, answers a different question, because the applicants already include the admitted: the two are linked by the admission rate, and both are drawn here.

Prediction · did the thirds of the entering class stop predicting who graduates?

The shrinking top third

The University sorts each entering freshman class into thirds by the grade point average it recalculates from the transcript, and publishes where the two boundaries fall. Because the thirds are cut fresh every year, the boundaries move: they are a direct reading of how the arriving class is distributed, taken by the University itself, with no model in between.

Two things happened at once, and the point of this section is that they happened together. The boundaries climbed toward the top of the scale, so the top third now occupies a sliver of grade point where it once occupied a fifth of a point. Meanwhile the students inside those thirds went on graduating at rates that stayed about as far apart as they ever were. The same amount of information about who finishes on time is now being carried by a much narrower strip of the ruler.

Calibration · how much does the answer depend on the choice of yardstick?

The yardstick bench

Grades rose. Whether that is inflation depends entirely on three choices, and reasonable people make them differently: which two periods to compare, which applicants to look at, and what to accept as an independent measure of how much students actually knew. Every one of those choices is a visible control here, and the figures show what survives each one.

Nothing here is fitted to produce an answer. With the achievement anchor set to none, the figure is the raw change; with an anchor on, the estimate moves by exactly the amount that anchor explains — no more.

Calibration · does the same grade mean the same thing at every school?

One school at a time

Statewide averages hide the thing people actually want to know, which is what happened at a school they recognize. This puts one high school’s grade record beside every independent measure of its students that exists, on a single axis of graduating classes.

Nothing is adjusted, ranked or scored. The measures simply sit next to each other, and where they part company is visible.

Achievement measures

Shown as the school’s position among all California high schools in that graduating class, so instruments with different scales can share one axis. Zero is the state average of schools. The state assessments test nearly everyone; the SAT, ACT and Advanced Placement measures cover only students who chose to sit them — where the two families part company at one school, that is usually a real and stable feature of the school, not error. At schools with few testers or few applicants, positions move a lot from year to year for reasons that have nothing to do with teaching.
