Reading high-school grades · methods and sources

How the figures are built

Back to the figures

The grade measure

The University of California publishes, for each California high school and each of its nine undergraduate campuses, the mean recalculated grade point average of that school’s applicants, of the subset admitted, and of the subset enrolled. It is the University’s own weighted, capped calculation over tenth- and eleventh-grade A–G coursework, not the school’s transcript average. The series runs from the graduating class of 1994 to the class of 2025 and covers 1,607 California high schools. Campus labels are as repaired on 30 July 2026.

A rise in the recalculated average can come from easier grading or from more, and more heavily weighted, coursework. Both are real changes in what a transcript says; neither is separable in these data. Every figure on the site is a school mean, not a distribution of students, and none of them describes students who never applied to the University.

The Universitywide pool

Alongside the nine campuses the University publishes a Universitywide figure: one aggregate over the whole system, in which a student who applies to several campuses is counted once. It is the pool used by default on the opening tab, because it is the closest thing in these data to “this school’s applicants to the University,” and because a single campus is a self-selected slice whose depth changes over time. It is labeled Universitywide throughout and is never a campus.

Class dating

Every year on every axis is a graduating class, which is also the University admission year: the class of 2025 applied in fall 2024 and entered in fall 2025. Each test is placed on the class that sat it — eleventh-grade tests are shifted forward one year, the tenth-grade examination two, and senior-year tests not at all.

Suppression

The University masks small cells. A school-campus-year with too few applicants, admitted or enrolled students reports no grade average; those cells are absent, not zero. The admitted average is masked more often than the applicant average, because admitted counts are smaller. The applicant thresholds in each sidebar trade coverage for stability; a school missing a figure in one class drops out of that class only. Wherever the site pools across classes, a class contributes only if the count and the average are both observed — a suppressed cell is never carried into an average as a zero, and a ratio is never formed from a numerator and a denominator observed in different years.

Why the campus-slice comparison holds the school constant

A school’s Berkeley applicants and its Merced applicants are drawn from one class in one year, taught by the same teachers under the same grading policy. Any change in what the school actually teaches, or in who enrolls there, moves both slices; only the depth of the slice differs, which is why that figure needs no test score to interpret. The slices are self-selected, though, and their composition can change: a campus whose applicant pool deepens will see its mean fall for that reason alone — which works against a rise, not for it. The readout on that tab reports how each pool’s depth changed.

The ceiling

The recalculated scale has a hard cap, so equal genuine improvement produces unequal measured gains at different starting points: a pool that already sits near the cap has less room to record any rise. A downward slope of change against starting level therefore measures compression — a loss of the resolution one grade point once carried — and is read here as that, not as proof of any particular cause.

The separation measure and the derived denied pool

The University publishes, for one high school, one campus and one graduating class, the applicant mean, the admitted mean and both counts. The denied group is the arithmetic remainder, so its mean follows exactly: with A applicants at mean gA and D admitted at mean gD, the R = A − D denied students sit at gR = (A·gA − D·gD) / R. The headline series on the crowded-top tab is the separation gD − gR, admitted minus denied: the distance the record actually contains between the students a campus took from a school and the students it turned away there. The older figure, admitted minus applicants, is linked to it exactly — gD − gA = (1 − p)·(gD − gR), where p is the admission rate — which is why it shrinks wherever admission rates are high even if nothing about grading changes. Both are drawn; the identity is asserted at machine precision in the verification chain.

The published means carry two decimals, so the derived denied mean inherits a rounding error amplified by A/R: the worst case for the separation is 0.005 × (1 + (A+D)/R). Cells below the denied-count threshold are withheld; every plotted separation carries its bound as a band; and in the separation-against-selectivity scatter a point whose bound exceeds 0.10 grade points is drawn hollow and left out of the fit rather than dropped silently. Because separation = gap / (1 − p), it climbs with the admission rate by arithmetic alone, so that scatter draws the curve the arithmetic would produce with the gap held constant beside the fitted tilt, and the subtitle reports both.

The prediction view on the same tab is a correlation across schools within one class: whether a school whose applicants carry higher grades gets a higher share of them admitted. It mixes what a campus rewards with where its applicants come from, and it is not a measure of any individual student’s chances. It is included because it is the quantity most often quoted, not because it is the cleanest.

The anchored estimate

For each high school, the yardstick-bench estimate is the change in its pool grade point average between two periods, minus the change in an achievement anchor multiplied by a rate: I = ΔG − β·ΔC. Setting the rate to zero returns the raw change. The rate is estimated from levels, not from changes: estimating it from the changes themselves would fit away the very thing being measured, so it is taken from the cross-section — how much higher a school’s pool average runs for each unit of measured achievement within one period — or set by hand.

Two nulls worth stating

Composition. The set of schools sending applicants changes over time, so the aggregate rise could in principle be schools entering and leaving the data rather than anything changing inside schools. It is not: recomputed over only the schools present in both windows, the default-window aggregate rise moves by −0.005 grade points. Every headline estimand on the site is within-school first for the same reason — a quantity built inside each school before schools are averaged cannot be moved by the mix of schools.

Course mix. Advanced Placement expansion is not what separates grade-rising schools from the rest: splitting schools by whether their Advanced Placement volume expanded, the anchored estimate is the same in both halves, and its correlation with the expansion is +0.04. More weighted coursework raises the recalculated average mechanically, but changes in how much of it schools offer do not track which schools rose.

The two references on the opening tab

The opening tab sets each school’s grade change against its change in one achievement anchor, and offers two lines to read that scatter against. The empirical relationship is the weighted least-squares line fitted to these very schools. It carries an intercept, so the average distance from it is zero by construction and only the spread of those distances is informative; what the line itself reports is its height where the anchor did not move, which is the part of the grade movement that no movement in measured achievement accompanies. Equal standardized movement is a different object: a line through the origin whose slope is the ratio of the two spreads, so a school that moved one standard deviation on the anchor is drawn as having moved one standard deviation on grades. That line is a claim stated in advance rather than one read off the data, so the average distance from it is a quantity in its own right. The two encode different assertions and are not interchangeable.

Neither line is a causal estimate. A school’s measured achievement and its grades are both consequences of what happened at the school, and a distance from either line names an unexplained residual, not a cause.

The achievement anchors

Two families of instrument, which are not the same. The census tests cover essentially every eleventh grader, including students who will not finish twelfth grade and never apply anywhere. The SAT, ACT and Advanced Placement measures cover only self-selected testers — much closer to the students who submit grade point averages to the University, but the share of a school sitting a test also changed over the years, which moves the school mean on its own. Across schools the two families correlate about 0.79, so a census anchor absorbs somewhat less of any real achievement change than a college-bound anchor would; their difference is itself a stable school trait, not noise.

One census instrument is used per class: the high-school exit examination for the classes of 2003 and 2015, the eleventh-grade standards test for 2004 to 2014, and the current state assessment from 2016 onward. Raw levels are not comparable across those seams; the standardized version — each school’s position within its own class and instrument — is, and the bench warns whenever the two chosen periods rest on different instruments. No census measure exists for the classes of 1994 to 2002 or 2021 and 2022, when the tests were not given or were not representative. The college-bound measures end with the class of 2020, when the state stopped publishing them. Standardized positions at schools with few testers or few applicants move a lot from year to year for reasons that have nothing to do with teaching.

Admission regimes

Test scores were required for admission through the class of 2020, optional for the class of 2021, and not considered from the class of 2022. Shaded bands mark this on every time axis. The change of regime is not the only thing that happened in those years, and nothing on this site is framed as evidence about test policy in either direction. The grade rise itself long predates those years: the anchored estimates for windows ending with the class of 2019 are positive everywhere the anchors reach, and the chain-linked index climbs through the whole record.

The share of high-need pupils

The school trait offered as a filter on the cross-school views is the state’s unduplicated count of pupils who are low-income, English learners, or foster youth, as a share of enrollment — a need measure broader than any single one of those categories, counted once per pupil. Two facts about it belong here rather than in a caveat box.

First, across schools, admission rates track it. In the pooled recent classes the correlation between a school’s measured achievement and its admission rate at the selective campuses largely vanishes when the share of high-need pupils is held fixed — at San Diego from −0.52 to −0.03, at Berkeley from −0.21 to +0.02 — so a cross-school gradient in admission outcomes on these pages should be read as tracking school need before it is read as tracking anything else. That is a statement about what admission decisions are keyed to across schools, not about any figure built within schools over time, which the mix of schools cannot move.

Second, that control does not transfer to outcomes. For a relationship between school achievement and what happens to students later — graduation, first-year college performance — the disadvantages the measure counts are part of the mechanism producing the relationship, not a nuisance lying across it. Holding need fixed there answers a narrower question — whether achievement still predicts among schools of similar need — and a gradient that shrinks under that restriction has been narrowed, not refuted. The filter is provided on the cross-school views because readers will ask; what is concluded from it differs by view, as above.

Corroboration from the other public system

The California State University publishes the mean high-school grade point average of its entering freshmen by campus. From fall 2021 through fall 2025 the campus means run from about 4.0 at San Luis Obispo down to about 3.1 at Dominguez Hills while the systemwide mean holds flat near 3.5 — the same picture the University of California’s campus slices give, read from a second system: pools of different depth sit at very different grade levels even as the whole moves little. The same source carries the counterweight this site states wherever it applies: within one CSU campus and one entering year, an additional point of high-school grade average predicts very nearly one additional point of first-year college grade average (a slope of about 1.0). Pooled across campuses that slope falls to about 0.7, entirely because more selective campuses grade harder. Where the scale still has room, a grade point average still predicts.

The thirds of the entering class

Tab 4 leaves the high-school data behind entirely. Its numbers are the University’s own Graduation Dashboard, “Grad. rates by GPA groups”: each entering freshman class is cut into three equal groups by the recalculated grade point average the University computes at entry, and the dashboard publishes the boundaries, the counts inside each group, and how many of those students had a degree after four, five and six years. The same boundaries are applied at every campus, which this build verified in all twelve cohort years.

Three points of care. The boundaries move. They are cut fresh from each class, so a band is an ordinal position in its own class, not a fixed standard: the top third of 2021 is not the same grade point average as the top third of 2010, and no comparison here assumes it is. Totals are a lower bound. The dashboard publishes no total-only view for these years; every figure arrives split by Pell Grant status, by first-generation status, or by ethnicity, and each split drops its own unknown cases. Totals here are the Pell Grant split summed, the least lossy of the three, and are labeled as a lower bound. Rates are unaffected, since each band’s rate is its own graduates over its own entrants. Cohorts are only shown at horizons they have reached. Six-year rates end with the class entering 2019 and five-year rates with 2020; those lines stop rather than falling. The classes entering in 2020 and 2021 met the pandemic while enrolled and are not comparable with earlier classes for reasons unrelated to grades.

Provenance of the campus labels

The University’s dashboard of grade averages by school reads its campus selection one position apart from its counts tab; read naively, every campus-named grade series comes out attached to the neighboring campus, and the systemwide aggregate comes out under Berkeley’s name. The offset was diagnosed on 25 July 2026 and the extraction pipeline repaired on 30 July 2026; every campus-named number on this site is from the repaired extract. A study circulated on 9 July 2026 predates the repair: its Universitywide figures were right, and every campus name attached to a number in it was not. The verification chain reproduces that study’s headline change (+0.0955; this build computes +0.0958) from the Universitywide pool, which is the pool the study was in fact describing.

Data inventory

Grades: University of California Information Center, freshman admissions by California high school — applicant, admitted and enrolled mean recalculated grade point averages and counts, by school, campus and Universitywide, graduating classes 1994–2025; 2026 extract, campus labels as repaired 30 July 2026. Graduation: University of California Graduation Dashboard (final rates), “Grad. rates by GPA groups”, freshman entry cohorts 2010–2021, all residencies, UCOP refresh of September 9, 2026 (installed October 2, 2026); six-year rates mature through the 2019 cohort, five-year through 2020. Census achievement: California Department of Education school research files — the current state assessment (used for the classes of 2016–2020 and 2023–2025), the eleventh-grade standards test (classes 2004–2014), and the high-school exit examination (classes 2003 and 2015). College-bound achievement: California Department of Education SAT, ACT and Advanced Placement school files, through the class of 2020, after which the state stopped publishing them. School traits: the share of high-need pupils is the state’s unduplicated pupil count (low-income, English learner, or foster youth), as a share of enrollment, from the state’s school funding data. Corroboration: California State University institutional dashboards, entering-freshman high-school grade averages by campus (fall 2021–2025) and first-year college grade averages (entering cohorts fall 2021–2024). Both data payloads beside this page were prepared on 8 August 2026.

Every page is self-contained apart from the two data files in the same folder: no server, no network, nothing loaded from elsewhere.
