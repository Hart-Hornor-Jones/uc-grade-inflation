Reading high-school grades · California, classes 1994–2025

Did high-school grades inflate, and does anyone still learn anything from them?

Nobody disputes that the grade point averages arriving at the University of California have risen. The open question is what the scale still communicates, and it splits into five questions that reasonable people weigh differently: whether grades have crowded against the top of the scale until one grade point distinguishes very little (compression); whether grades moved away from independent measures of the same students (criterion divergence); whether admission decisions now express distinctions the scale no longer states (selection divergence); whether a grade means the same thing at different schools and in different years (calibration); and whether the grade a student enters with still predicts what happens afterward (prediction). Each tab takes up one of these as a question, not a verdict, and hands the judgments that drive the answer — which years to compare, which students to count, what to accept as independent evidence of achievement — to the reader as controls.

Everything is built from the University’s published grade point averages for every California high school, graduating classes 1994 to 2025, set beside every school-level testing program the state has run since 1999, and from the University’s own record of who graduates. The rise these figures examine runs back decades — it neither starts nor breaks at any single policy year — and the long axes are drawn in full so that can be seen.
